1. Why knitwear is costed differently

If you have costed a woven garment before, you started with a fabric price per metre and worked out how many metres the pattern needed. Knitwear does not work that way, because in knitwear you are not buying fabric — you are buying yarn and making the fabric yourself, at the same moment you make the garment.

Three consequences follow, and they shape this whole tool:

  1. Yarn is sold by weight, not by length or area. So the question is never “how many metres?” but “how many grams?” You weigh the panels.
  2. Your machine cost is knitting time, not sewing time. A knitting machine occupied for 50 minutes costs you 50 minutes of that machine, its operator, its power and its floor space — whether it is making something simple or something spectacular.
  3. How you choose to construct the garment changes how much yarn you throw away — anywhere from almost nothing to a fifth of what you bought. That is a design decision with a price on it.

2. Yarn: count, gauge, and why they have to match

Count tells you how thick a yarn is. This tool uses the metric count, Nm: the number of 1000 m lengths of that yarn that weigh one kilogram. So Nm 28 means 28 km of yarn per kg. The counter-intuitive part: a higher Nm is a finer yarn, because you need more length of a thin yarn to reach a kilo.

You will see counts written as 2/28Nm. That means two ends of Nm 28 twisted together, which gives a resultant count of Nm 14 — twice as heavy per unit length as a single end. The tool asks you for the resultant count.

Gauge (written GG) is a property of the machine, not the yarn: the number of needles per inch. A 3GG machine has thick needles set far apart and needs a chunky yarn. A 12GG machine has fine needles set close together and needs a fine one.

They have to match, and the tool will tell you when they don't. Put a chunky yarn on a fine machine and it jams, or breaks needles. Put a fine yarn on a coarse machine and you get a slack, holey, unsaleable fabric. This is the single most common mistake in student knitwear specifications, so the tool checks it live and explains what it found.

Yarn price varies more than almost anything else in fashion. Cashmere can be ten times the price of a wool blend per kilo. For most knitwear yarn is the largest single cost line — which is why knitwear sits where it does on a price list, and why yarn choice, not clever making, is what really sets your cost.

Finally, yarn wastage is not the same as cutting waste. Even with no cutting at all you lose yarn to setting up the machine, tension runs, thread-ups, knit-downs and mending. Industry practice is to add at least 10% before any cutting waste on top.

This is where costing and sustainability stop being separate subjects. Cut and sew is cheaper per garment and throws away roughly a fifth of the yarn you paid for. Fully fashioned wastes almost nothing and costs more, because linking is skilled work. There is no answer at the back of the book. You will have to take a position and defend it — and this tool gives you both numbers, in pounds, to defend it with.

4. The cost ladder

Every costing in the industry climbs the same ladder. The names matter because buyers and suppliers use them precisely.

  1. Works cost (also CMT: cut, make and trim). Yarn + knitting + making-up + finishing + trims. What it physically costs to produce the garment.
  2. FOB (free on board). Works cost + the factory's overheads, its allowance for seconds, its profit, and any agent's commission. This is the price the factory quotes you.
  3. Landed cost (LCP). FOB + freight + insurance + import duty + customs clearance + transport to your warehouse. What the garment has cost by the time you can actually touch it. Every retail price decision is made from this number, not from the works cost.
  4. Retail price. Landed cost + the costs of running your business + your profit, then VAT on top. How you get from landed to retail depends entirely on what kind of business you are — which is section 9.

5. Margin and markup are not the same thing

Students lose marks on this constantly, and so do real businesses.

 FormulaExample: cost £40, sells for £100
Margin(price − cost) ÷ price60 ÷ 100 = 60%
Markup(price − cost) ÷ cost60 ÷ 40 = 150%

Same garment, same money, two very different-looking percentages. Margin is a share of what the customer paid; markup is a multiple of what you paid. Retailers usually talk in margin. Wholesalers often talk in multiples. Always check which one you are being quoted, and note that margins are always calculated excluding VAT — the VAT was never your money.

6. Four business models, four different prices

This is the part people find surprising. The same garment, with exactly the same landed cost, will carry four different retail prices depending on who is selling it and how. Not because anyone is being greedy, but because each model has to cover different costs.

Designer‑maker (you make it and sell it yourself)
There is no factory margin, because you are the factory. But there is a trap here that catches nearly everyone: you must pay yourself. If you cost your own knitting and finishing time at zero, your costing will look wonderful and your business will quietly fail, because you cannot scale a business that only works when the labour is free. The tool makes you put a rate on your own hours.
Direct‑to‑consumer brand (your own website)
Typically targets a 55–65% margin, noticeably higher than wholesale. That is not a bigger prize — it is a bigger job. That margin has to fund the cost of acquiring each customer, a return rate that can run 25–40%, the shipping you advertised as free, and someone answering the emails. The tool shows those as separate lines so you can see the higher margin being spent.
Wholesale brand (you sell to shops, they sell to the public)
You set a wholesale price; the retailer multiplies it to get the retail price. Doubling it is called keystone; most retailers now want a little more, around 2.2 to 2.5 times. The practical test: your wholesale price needs to land at roughly 40–60% of the final retail price. Above 60% the shop cannot make its own margin and will not buy. Below 40% you are giving away money that should have been yours.
High street / volume retail (own‑brand, sourced directly)
Introduces the idea students most often miss: the margin you planned is not the margin you get. The margin you set when you buy is the intake margin. Then some of the stock does not sell at full price, and gets marked down. What is left after the markdowns is the achieved margin, and that is the real one. Plan 62% intake, sell only 60% at full price and the rest at 40% off, and your achieved margin falls a long way. This is why volume retail chases volume and low unit costs so hard.

7. Responsible design as a costing decision

Sustainability in a costing exercise is often a box-ticking round at the end. It is not one here, because none of it is free and all of it moves the numbers. Four ideas do most of the work:

Cost per wear. Divide the retail price by the number of times the garment will realistically be worn. A well-made lambswool jumper at £180 that gets worn 200 times costs 90p a wear. A £25 acrylic one worn 15 times costs £1.67 — nearly twice as much per wear, at a seventh of the price. This is the argument for durability that a buyer will actually listen to, because it is an economic argument rather than a moral one.

Waste is money. The tool shows wasted yarn in grams and in pounds, per garment and across the whole order. A waste percentage is easy to shrug off. A number with a pound sign in front of it is harder to ignore.

Mono-material beats blends at end of life. A garment that is one fibre can be recycled back into fibre. Blends usually cannot be separated, so they are downcycled or burnt. But there is a genuine tension here rather than a clean answer: adding a little elastane makes a garment hold its shape and last longer in use, while making it much harder to recycle at the end. The tool will show you both sides moving. Deciding is your job.

Designing for a long life costs money up front. A repair allowance, a spare hank of yarn in the box, reinforced elbows, proper care instructions, a take-back scheme — each has a price, and each appears in this tool as a line item you have to justify against your margin. Learning to make that case is the point.

Check yourself before you start

  1. Which is the finer yarn, Nm 8 or Nm 28?
    Answer

    Nm 28. A higher metric count means more length per kilogram, so the yarn is thinner.

  2. A garment costs you £30 landed and sells for £90 excluding VAT. What is the margin, and what is the markup?
    Answer

    Margin is 60 ÷ 90 = 66.7%. Markup is 60 ÷ 30 = 200%. Same £60 of profit, two different percentages.

  3. You switch a design from cut and sew to fully fashioned. Name one number that goes up and one that goes down.
    Answer

    Assembly cost goes up, because linking is slower and more skilled than sewing. Wasted yarn goes down sharply, because nothing is cut away. Whether the total rises or falls depends on how expensive your yarn is — and that is exactly the judgement the tool is asking you to make.

  4. Why does a direct-to-consumer brand need a higher margin than a wholesale brand?
    Answer

    Because it is paying for things wholesale never sees: winning each customer, handling returns, shipping single orders, and customer service. The extra margin is not extra profit — it is funding extra work.

What you would like it to sell for. The tool will tell you later whether your design supports it.

Why does quantity change the cost of a garment?

Two reasons. First, some costs are fixed per order rather than per garment — programming the knitting machine, knitting a sample, setting up. Spread over 50 units those hurt; spread over 2,000 they almost vanish. Second, yarn is cheaper per kilo in quantity, and spinners have their own minimums, often by dye lot.

This is why a designer-maker's costing and a high street costing for a visually similar jumper can be so far apart. It is rarely that one is making it better.

Construction method

Where will it be made?

This switches section 8 between a short domestic transport cost and a full import calculation.

Why does this belong in a discussion about responsible design?

Because it cuts both ways, and you should be able to argue it properly rather than assume local is automatically better.

Making near to your market shortens freight, makes shorter runs and repeat orders viable, lets you visit the factory and see conditions for yourself, and cuts the working capital tied up in stock on a ship. Against that, the UK has lost much of its knitwear capacity, hourly rates are higher, and a factory abroad with excellent certification and renewable power may genuinely have a lower footprint per garment than a domestic one on a dirty grid. Freight is usually a small share of a garment's total impact — unless it goes by air, which changes the picture completely.

The defensible position is a specific one, with reasons. Not a slogan.

Why is a percentage of wasted yarn worth taking seriously?

Because it is the cost line you are least likely to notice and most able to change. Yarn is usually the largest single cost in a knitted garment, so a fifth of it thrown away is a fifth of your biggest cost line, gone. Across a 300-unit order that is real money — the dashboard shows you exactly how much.

It is also the clearest example of a decision that is simultaneously commercial and environmental, with no conflict between the two. Cutting waste out saves money and reduces impact at the same time. Those decisions are rarer than you would hope, so when you find one, take it.

What is actually inside a machine hour rate?

Not just wages. An hour of knitting machine costs the operator's time, the machine's depreciation (an industrial flat-knitting machine is a serious capital purchase), the electricity, the floor space it stands on, its maintenance, and the technician who programmes and services it. That is why the rate is well above an hourly wage, and why factories care so much about keeping machines running.

It also explains something that puzzles people about knitwear pricing: an all-over cable can cost noticeably more than a plain jumper in the same yarn, with no extra material at all. You are paying for machine time. A plain panel might knit in around 15 minutes; a heavy cable can take 45 to 60, partly because the machine must be slowed down to protect the needles. Choosing a stitch is a costing decision.

Section 5

Making‑up and finishing

Turning panels into a garment, and then turning a garment into a good one. Two things here are routinely left out of student costings and never left out of a real one: mending and wet finishing.

Assembly

£0.00
Why is linking so much more expensive than sewing?

A linker joins two knitted panels stitch by stitch, putting each individual loop from one panel onto a point on the linking machine so it meets its counterpart exactly. It is slow, it needs good eyesight and years of practice, and it cannot be rushed without showing.

What you get for it is a seam that is flat and stable rather than bulky, which is why good knitwear sits properly on the shoulder and cheap knitwear does not. It is also a skill in short supply in the UK, which is part of why domestic knitwear costs what it does. When you specify fully fashioned, this is the cost you are accepting in exchange for almost no waste and a better garment.

Why can't wet finishing be skipped to save money?

Because a knitted garment straight off the machine is not finished — it is tight, uneven and lifeless. Washing relaxes the stitches, lets the yarn bloom and settles the garment to its true measurements. Wool that has not been milled feels thin and papery; the same wool washed properly feels like the jumper you would actually buy.

Skip it and you have not saved 80p. You have made something that will not sell, and that will change size the first time your customer washes it — which is how a garment ends up in a bin after three wears. Wet finishing is one of the cheapest durability interventions available to you.

What does “removable” have to do with recycling?

Fibre-to-fibre recycling needs a clean, single-fibre input. A wool jumper with polyester woven labels, a nylon zip and metal buttons is not a wool input — it is a mixture, and someone has to take it apart by hand before it can be recycled at all. At scale, that hand work usually means it simply is not done.

So trims that come off easily, or are made of the same fibre as the garment, are worth real money at end of life. Printing care details directly onto the garment, choosing a wool label, or using a single-fibre thread costs pennies now and decides whether the garment is recyclable later. This is what “design for disassembly” means in practice, and it feeds the circularity index in section 10.

Why is margin worked out on the selling price and not on cost?

Convention, and it matters. A factory quoting “15% margin” means 15% of what it charges you, not 15% added to what it spent. So the sum is FOB = (cost of production + seconds) ÷ (1 − margin% − commission%) — you divide rather than multiply.

The difference is not small. On £20 of cost, adding 25% gives £25.00, but a 25% margin on the selling price gives £26.67. If you multiply when you should divide, you will under-quote every job, and the gap widens as margins rise. Get this one right and you will already be ahead of a lot of people working in industry.

Why does every pricing decision start from landed cost?

Because landed cost is the last honest number before opinion starts. It is what the garment has genuinely cost you, with nothing hopeful in it.

The classic mistake is to set a retail price from the factory quote, because that is the figure in the email. Then freight, duty and clearance arrive and quietly eat the margin that was supposed to pay for everything else. On a £20 FOB garment, 12% duty plus freight and clearance can easily add £3 — and at a 2.5× retail multiple that is £7.50 of retail price you forgot to charge.

The elastane problem, and why there is no clean answer

A few per cent of elastane makes a knitted garment hold its shape, recover after wearing, and stay wearable for longer. That is a real durability gain, and durability is the single biggest lever on a garment's footprint, because the impact is spread over more wears.

But elastane cannot currently be separated from wool at scale. A 97% wool, 3% elastane jumper is not a wool input for recycling — it is a blend, and blends get downcycled into insulation or burnt for energy. So the elastane that extends the garment's life in use is what closes off its options at end of life.

Both of those are true at once. The tool shows both moving and does not resolve it for you, because the resolution depends on the garment: for a fitted rib that will be worn twice a week, the elastane may well be worth it; for a loose lambswool crew that never needed it, it is a recyclable garment given away for nothing. Your job is to know which one you are designing, and say so.
